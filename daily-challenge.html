Freelancer finance daily challenge

One question a day for 30 days. Each answer is short and practical — read it, then check whether your own setup already does what it says. Start on any day; the order does not matter. The answers are general guidance, not tax or legal advice — check your local rules where a number or a law is involved.

The 30 days

Day 1 — What is the first thing to put on every invoice?

An invoice number, the date, the due date, the amount, and how to pay. Everything else — line items, your business details, tax — matters, but without those five, the client cannot pay you even if they want to.

Day 2 — When is an invoice overdue?

The day after the due date. An invoice due on the 15th is overdue on the 16th, not "a bit late" — that is the point of having a due date at all. A tracker with a status column built from the due date will flip to Overdue on its own, so you never have to remember.

Day 3 — How long should payment terms be: Net 7, 14, or 30?

Shorter is better for your cash flow, and Net 14 is a common middle ground. Net 30 is the most common term clients expect, so if you go shorter, say so on the quote as well as the invoice — a surprise due date is the fastest way to a late payment.

Day 4 — What do you do 3 days before an invoice is due?

Send a friendly heads-up: the invoice is due in 3 days, here is the amount, here is the payment link. Most unpaid invoices are forgotten, not refused, and a reminder before the due date catches the ones that would have drifted.

Day 5 — An invoice is 1 day overdue. What now?

Send the Day 1 email: assume it was forgotten, attach the invoice again, and ask for a payment date. Keep it short and polite — this is still a reminder, not a complaint, and most late invoices get paid at this step.

Day 6 — 7 days overdue: what changes?

The tone firms up. Restate the due date, the amount, and the payment link, and ask for a specific payment date instead of "soon". You are no longer reminding — you are asking for a commitment, in writing.

Day 7 — 14 days overdue: what do you add?

If your terms allow it, add the late fee and state the new total. The fee is not punishment, it is the price your terms already set for late payment — and stating it makes the next email easier to write.

Day 8 — 30 days overdue: what is the final notice?

A final notice that states the amount, the days overdue, and what happens next — pausing work, handing the debt to a collection service, or a small claim. What "next" means depends on your country, so check your local rules before you write it.

Day 9 — How do you calculate a late fee?

Amount × rate × days overdue, divided by 365. A $1,200 invoice at 12% a year, 30 days late, is $11.84. The late-fee calculator does the arithmetic — you only need the amount, the due date, and the rate from your terms.

Day 10 — What late-fee rate can you charge?

Whatever your terms state and your local rules allow. The rate has to be written down before the work starts, not invented after the invoice is late — a fee the client never agreed to is a dispute waiting to happen.

Day 11 — Should you charge a deposit?

Yes, for new clients. 50% upfront is common: it filters out clients who were never going to pay, and it means the worst case is half the job unpaid instead of all of it. State the deposit on the quote, not only on the invoice.

Day 12 — What should a quote include?

The scope of work, the price, how long the quote stays valid, the deposit, and the payment terms. A quote without a validity date is an open-ended promise — and a quote without payment terms is an invoice that will be late.

Day 13 — How long should a quote stay valid?

14 or 30 days. After that, re-quote: your costs, your availability, and the client's needs may all have changed, and honouring a stale price is how a profitable job turns into a loss.

Day 14 — Hourly or fixed price?

Hourly when the scope is unclear or the client keeps changing it; fixed when the deliverable is defined and you can estimate it. Fixed price pays better when you are fast, hourly pays better when the job grows — pick the one that matches the risk.

Day 15 — How do you raise your rates?

Tell existing clients in advance, tie the rise to a date, and apply it to new work first. A short note — "from March 1 my rate is X" — beats a surprise on the next invoice, and clients who value the work will stay.

Day 16 — How do you price by replacement cost?

Ask what it would cost the client to replace you: hire someone else, do it themselves, or leave the work undone. If that number is higher than your price, you are undercharging — and the client already knows it.

Day 17 — What is a retainer?

A regular fee, usually monthly, that reserves your time or guarantees your availability. It turns lumpy project income into a baseline you can plan around, and it is the closest thing freelancing has to a salary.

Day 18 — How much tax should you set aside from each payment?

Move a fixed percentage into a separate account the moment each payment lands — before you spend any of it. The right percentage depends on your country, your income, and what you can deduct, so check your local rules and pick a number you will actually stick to.

Day 19 — What counts as a business expense?

Money you spend to run the business: software, travel, equipment, a coworking desk. If it is for a client, log it as reimbursable so it comes back; if it is for you, log it as an expense so it counts at tax time. Keep the receipt either way.

Day 20 — How do you track expenses without drowning in them?

One row per expense — date, category, vendor, amount, receipt yes or no — and a summary that totals by category. The habit is weekly, not monthly: ten minutes on a Friday beats an afternoon of reconstructing a whole month from bank statements.

Day 21 — What is a purchase order?

The client's formal document authorising the work at an agreed price. If a client sends one, keep it with the quote: it is your proof of what was agreed, and it is what their accounts department will look for before they pay.

Day 22 — What is scope creep, and how do you stop it?

Work growing beyond what was agreed, without a matching change to the price. You stop it with a written scope of work and a change order for anything extra — "yes, and here is what it costs" instead of a quiet yes that eats your margin.

Day 23 — What is a change order?

A written change to the agreed scope of work, usually with a price change attached. It protects both sides: the client gets the extra work they asked for, and you get paid for it instead of absorbing it.

Day 24 — Should you ever work without a contract?

No. Even a short written agreement — scope, price, payment terms, who owns the work — beats a verbal one, because memory is the first thing that fails when a payment is late. This is practical advice, not legal advice.

Day 25 — What is a credit note?

A document that reduces what a client owes — for a refund, a corrected invoice, or work that was not delivered. It keeps your books honest: the original invoice stays on record, and the credit note shows what changed and why.

Day 26 — A client asks for a discount. What do you do?

Decide in advance when you will say yes — early payment, bulk work, a long retainer — and when you will say no. Never discount by quietly shrinking the scope: if the price drops, the deliverable drops with it, in writing.

Day 27 — A client always pays late. What changes?

Shorten their terms, require a deposit, and charge the late fee your terms already allow. If it never improves, stop taking their work — a client who pays late every time costs more in chasing than the job is worth.

Day 28 — What is accounts receivable?

Money clients owe you for work you have already delivered and invoiced. It is the number on your summary sheet that tells you how much of your income exists only on paper — and the chase is what turns it into money in the bank.

Day 29 — How often should you check who owes you?

Once a week, ten minutes: filter the tracker for Overdue, send the next chase email for each one, update the Last Chased date, and note the next action. The weekly habit is the whole system — the tracker and the emails are just the tools it uses.

Day 30 — What is the one habit that gets freelancers paid?

Invoice the same day you deliver, with a due date and a payment link on it. Every day between delivery and invoicing is a day the client forgets the work, and a late invoice starts its life already late.
