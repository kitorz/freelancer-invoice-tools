The late-payment chase guide

Most unpaid invoices are not refused — they are forgotten. A short, predictable sequence of reminders fixes that. This guide describes the cadence and the routine; the ready-to-send email wording and the tracker live in the kit linked at the bottom.

The 5-email chase cadence

Each step is one short email. Send it yourself — nothing in this system goes out under your name without you seeing it. Every email should state the invoice number, the amount, the due date, and the payment link, so the client never has to search for anything.

  1. 3 days before the due date — the heads-up A polite reminder before anything is late. The point is to catch the invoice while it is still easy to pay: confirm the client received it, confirm the amount, and give the payment link again. Most clients pay at this step.
  2. Day 1 overdue — firm and friendly The due date has passed. Restate the amount and the payment link, and ask for payment or a reply by a specific date. Keep the tone professional — this is a reminder, not an accusation.
  3. Day 7 overdue — a short reminder Same message, new date. Short is fine: the client already has the details, they need a nudge and a deadline, not a new essay.
  4. Day 14 overdue — firmer, with a clear next step Say plainly what happens next if payment does not arrive — for example, that further work pauses until the account is settled. Give a concrete date. Do not threaten anything you are not prepared to do.
  5. Day 30 overdue — the final notice The last email in the sequence. Restate the total owed, including any late fee your terms allow (see the late-fee calculator), and state what you will do next. Still professional, no threats.

The weekly 10-minute routine

The cadence only works if someone runs it every week. Pick one fixed slot — Friday works well — and do the same four things, in the same order, every week:

  1. Open your tracker and filter for Overdue invoices.
  2. For each one, send the next email in the sequence — never the same email twice, never two steps at once.
  3. Update Last Chased with today's date, so you always know when you last wrote.
  4. Note the Next Action — the date and the step that comes next.

Ten minutes, same order, every week. That is the whole system: the tracker remembers what you would otherwise have to remember, and the routine makes the follow-up happen instead of slipping another week.

After day 30: what then?

If the full cadence runs and the invoice is still unpaid, stop sending the same reminders. The usual next options, in order of escalation, are:

  • Pause further work for that client until the account is settled — and say so in writing before you do it.
  • Offer a payment plan in writing: fixed amounts, fixed dates, and what happens if a payment is missed. Get the client's agreement in writing.
  • Escalate formally — a written demand, a collections agency, or legal advice. Which one fits depends on the amount and your jurisdiction; this guide cannot answer that, and a short consultation with a local professional is cheaper than guessing.

Whatever you do, keep records: every email, every reply, every promised date. If a dispute ever goes further, that paper trail is what matters.

Three mistakes that cost freelancers money

  • Waiting for the client to remember. Invoices do not pay themselves. The first reminder before the due date is the cheapest email in the whole sequence.
  • Chasing from memory. Without a tracker, you cannot answer the basic question: who owes me, how much, since when, and what did I last send? That is exactly what slips.
  • Going silent out of awkwardness. A short, polite, factual reminder is part of doing business. Clients expect it; the awkward part is the silence afterwards, not the email.
