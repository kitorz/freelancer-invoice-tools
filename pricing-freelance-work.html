Hourly vs fixed: how to price freelance work

The pricing question is not which model is better in general — it is which one fits this job, this client, and how well you can predict the work. Get that decision right and the rest is arithmetic.

When hourly fits

Charge by the hour when the scope is genuinely uncertain:

  • Open-ended work. Ongoing support, maintenance, research, or “help us fix this as it comes up” — work with no defined end point.
  • A new kind of job. If you have never done this exact thing before, you cannot predict how long it takes. Hourly pricing moves that risk to the client, where it belongs while you are learning.
  • A client who changes their mind. If the requirements move every week, an hourly rate means every change is paid for. A fixed price means every change is argued about.

The trade-off is real: hourly work pays you for time, not for speed. The faster you get, the less you earn per job — unless you raise the rate to match. Keep a simple log of hours per task; it is also the evidence that makes your invoice hard to dispute.

When fixed fits

Charge a fixed price when the job is well defined:

  • Repeatable work. You have done this ten times and you know it takes about six hours. A fixed price turns your experience into profit instead of a pay cut.
  • A clear deliverable. “Four pages of website copy” or “a logo with three revisions” — the client knows what they get, you know what you do, and the price is one number.
  • A client who wants certainty. Many clients prefer a fixed price because they can approve it once and stop thinking about it. That preference is worth something — price it in.

Fixed pricing only works with a written scope: what is included, how many revisions, and what counts as extra. Without that, “just one more small change” will eat the whole margin. Put the scope in the quote before the work starts.

Price by replacement cost, not by guessing

Whatever model you choose, set the number by what it would cost the client to get the same result another way — not by what feels modest, and not by what the last freelancer charged.

  • What would it cost them to do it themselves? Hours of their own time, at their own hourly value, plus the learning curve. If the answer is more than your price, your price is a bargain.
  • What would an agency charge? Not to copy the number, but to know the ceiling. Your price should sit between “cheaper than doing it badly themselves” and “cheaper than an agency”.
  • What does it cost you? Your time, your tools, and the time you spend chasing payment and doing admin. A rate that ignores admin time is a rate that pays you less than you think.

As a sanity check, work backwards: take the fixed price, divide it by the hours you expect, and ask whether the hourly equivalent is a rate you would accept. If it is not, the fixed price is too low.

Raising your rates

Rates that never rise are rates that fall in real terms. A few rules that make increases painless:

  • Raise for new clients first. New clients have no old price to compare against. Existing clients get the new rate at the next project, with notice — not mid-project.
  • Give notice in writing. “From 1 July, my rate for this work is $90/hour, up from $75.” One sentence, one date, no apology and no long explanation.
  • Raise after proof, not before. The easiest moment is right after a job the client loved, or when your calendar is full. A full calendar is the market telling you the price is too low.
  • Expect some clients to leave. That is not failure — it is the filter working. A client who leaves over a fair increase was pricing you as a cost, not as a result.

One honest note: no pricing method guarantees a particular income. Pricing well improves your odds and your margins; it cannot create demand that is not there.

Price it, then make sure it gets paid

A good price means little if the invoice sits unpaid. Whatever rate you agree, put it in writing on the quote, repeat it on the invoice with a due date and payment terms, and follow up on a fixed schedule if the date passes.
