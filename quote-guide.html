How to write a quote that gets paid

A quote is not just a price. It is the document that decides whether a late payment is a misunderstanding or a breach of terms. Write it well and the chase — if you ever need one — writes itself.

What every quote should include

  • Scope, in plain words. What exactly you will deliver, and what you will not. Vague scope is the most common source of unpaid extra work.
  • Deliverables and format. Files, formats, quantities — "10 social graphics, PNG, 1080×1080" beats "social media design".
  • Price and what it covers. A single total, with the line items behind it. If you use the invoice total calculator, the same breakdown carries straight onto the invoice.
  • Payment terms. Deposit, due date, and how payment is made. Net 14 or Net 30 should be written down, not assumed.
  • Validity period. "This quote is valid for 14 days." Prices and availability change; a quote without an expiry date is an open promise.
  • Revision limit. How many rounds of changes are included, and what extra rounds cost. Unlimited revisions are the fastest way to lose money on a fixed price.
  • Late-fee note. One line stating that overdue amounts carry a late fee at a stated annual rate. Without it, charging one later is much harder.

Pricing by replacement cost

Do not price by guessing what the client might accept. Price by what it would cost the client to get the same result without you:

  • Do it themselves: how many hours would it take them, at their own hourly value, plus the learning curve and the mistakes?
  • Hire someone else: what would an agency or another freelancer charge for the same deliverable?
  • Leave it undone: what does the missing piece cost them — a launch delayed, a campaign without assets, a report nobody writes?

Your price should sit below the cost of those alternatives and above the cost of your time. That gap is the value you are selling. When a client says the price is high, the answer is never to cut it silently — it is to show the comparison, or to shrink the scope until the price fits.

A useful rule for fixed prices: quote the project, not the hours. Clients buy an outcome; hours are your cost, not their value. If a job takes you half the time it would take a beginner, that is a reason to charge more per hour, not less.

Structuring the price

  • Deposit up front. A 30–50% deposit before work starts filters out clients who were never going to pay, and it means you are never more than half an invoice away from being paid.
  • Milestones for larger jobs. Split payment across agreed checkpoints — brief approved, draft delivered, final files delivered — instead of one lump at the end.
  • One price, one scope. If the client wants more, write a new quote for the extra work. Do not fold it into the original price and hope to be thanked.
  • State the payment method. Bank transfer, PayPal, Wise — name it, and put the payment link or details in the invoice when the time comes, not buried in an email thread.

Quote vs. estimate — say which one it is

A quote is a fixed price: you commit to delivering the stated scope for the stated amount. An estimate is a range: the price may move if the scope moves. Label the document correctly. Calling an estimate a quote locks you into a price for work that has not been fully scoped yet — and calling a quote an estimate makes a decisive client hesitate for no reason.

After the quote is sent

A quote that gets no reply is not a rejection — it is usually a forgotten email. Follow up once after 3–4 working days with a single short message: confirm they received it, ask if anything needs clarifying, and restate the validity date. If the quote expires, say so and offer to re-quote. And once a client accepts, send the invoice with the same numbers, the same terms, and the same due date — consistency between quote and invoice is what makes the chase guide work if payment ever slips.
