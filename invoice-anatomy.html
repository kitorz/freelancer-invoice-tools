What every freelancer invoice must include

An invoice is not a polite request for money — it is the document a client’s accounts department needs in order to pay you. If a field is missing, the invoice goes to the bottom of their pile while someone emails you to ask for it. Include everything below, every time.

The fields, in order

  1. Your details Your name or business name, email, and phone number. The client needs to reach you, and their records need a name that matches the payment. If you are registered for tax, add your tax number — check your local rules for what is required where you live.
  2. Client details The client’s name or company name, and the name of the person who approved the work. Invoices addressed to a company with no contact person are the ones that get lost between departments.
  3. Invoice number A unique number for every invoice, in sequence: INV-001, INV-002, and so on. Never reuse a number, and never skip one. Both you and the client will refer to it in every email about this payment.
  4. Dates The invoice date and the due date, written out in full — “Issued 1 June 2026, due 15 June 2026”. A due date is what turns a request into a deadline.
  5. Line items One line per piece of work: what was done, the quantity or hours, the rate, and the line total. “Website copywriting — 4 pages — $150/page — $600” beats “Work — $600” every time, because nobody disputes a line they can read.
  6. Totals Subtotal, any discount, any tax, and the total due — each on its own line, with the currency stated. If the total is hard to add up from the line items, the client will ask, and the payment waits. The invoice-total calculator builds the arithmetic for you, line by line, with discount and tax.
  7. Payment instructions How to pay, exactly: the method, the link or account details, and the currency. Put the payment link in the email and on the invoice — the client should never have to ask how to pay you.
  8. Payment terms The due date again, plus the late fee if you charge one, and any deposit already paid and deducted. If you need help writing this block, see the guide to payment terms on an invoice.

Five mistakes that delay payment

  • No invoice number. Without one, the client cannot reference the payment, and you cannot chase it. Every follow-up email starts with the number.
  • One lump-sum line. “Design work — $1,500” invites a question; five readable lines invite a payment.
  • No due date. An invoice without a due date is a suggestion. Always write the date out in full.
  • Payment details buried or missing. If the client has to reply to ask how to pay, you have added a week to your own wait.
  • Sending it late. An invoice sent three weeks after the work is an invoice that gets paid three weeks late. Send it the day the work is delivered — or the day the milestone is reached.

After you send it

Sending the invoice is the start, not the end. Record it in a tracker the same day — client, invoice number, amount, due date, status — so you always know who owes you, how much, and since when. When the due date passes, the follow-up sequence in the late-payment chase guide takes over: one short reminder before the date, then day 1, day 7, day 14, and day 30.
