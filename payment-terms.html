How to write payment terms on an invoice

Payment terms are the short block on your invoice that says when the money is due, how the client pays, and what happens if they pay late. Write them once, use them on every invoice, and most late payments never happen.

What payment terms should include

Five things belong in every payment-terms block. If any one is missing, the client has a reason — real or invented — to delay.

  • The due date. A calendar date, not just “Net 30”. Write both: “Due 30 June 2026 (Net 30)”. The date removes all argument about when the clock started.
  • The late fee. The rate and how it is calculated — for example, “1% per month on the unpaid balance”. Before charging one, check your local rules: some places cap late fees or require them to be agreed in advance. If you want a number to work with, the late-fee calculator does the arithmetic.
  • Accepted payment methods. Name them: bank transfer, PayPal, Wise. Include the link or account detail the client actually needs, so payment is one click, not a reply asking how.
  • The currency. State it on the invoice even if it seems obvious — “USD 1,200”, not “1,200”. Cross-border clients will otherwise pay in whatever currency is easiest for them.
  • What the payment covers. One line linking the invoice to the work: the project name, the invoice number, and the period. It also protects you if a client later claims they paid for something else.

Net 7, Net 14, Net 30: example wording

“Net” means the number of days the client has to pay after the invoice date. Shorter terms are normal for small, fast jobs; longer terms are normal for larger clients with slow accounts departments. Pick one and use it consistently.

  • Net 7 — “Payment due within 7 days of the invoice date. Due 8 July 2026.” Good for small fixed-price jobs and first-time clients.
  • Net 14 — “Payment due within 14 days of the invoice date. Due 15 July 2026.” A middle ground that most small businesses accept without a second thought.
  • Net 30 — “Payment due within 30 days of the invoice date. Due 31 July 2026.” Standard for larger companies, but it means you are effectively lending them a month of work — price accordingly, or ask for a deposit.

Whatever term you choose, put the same wording on the quote before the work starts, not only on the invoice after it. Terms agreed after the work is done are much harder to enforce — and if a client asks for Net 60, that is a negotiation, not a default: ask for a deposit in return.

Deposit terms

For new clients and larger jobs, ask for part of the fee before you start. A common split is 50% upfront and 50% on delivery, or 30% / 40% / 30% across milestones for longer projects. Write it the same way every time:

  • “A 50% deposit of USD 600 is due before work begins. The remaining USD 600 is due on delivery, Net 14.”
  • “Work begins when the deposit clears, not when the invoice is sent.”
  • State whether the deposit is refundable and under what conditions. “Non-refundable after work begins” is normal — but again, check your local rules before writing it.

A deposit does two jobs at once: it filters out clients who were never going to pay, and it means the worst case is a half-paid invoice, not a fully unpaid one.

What to do when the terms are ignored

Terms on paper do not collect themselves. When the due date passes with no payment:

  1. Day 1: send a short reminder with the invoice number, amount, due date, and payment link. Most late invoices are forgotten, not refused.
  2. Day 7: one short follow-up with a new date. Same details, no new essay.
  3. Day 14: state plainly what happens next — for example, that further work pauses until the account is settled.
  4. Day 30: a final notice, including any late fee your terms allow.

That sequence — with the exact timing and the routine that keeps it running — is described in the late-payment chase guide. The point is to be predictable: the client should know the next email is coming, and when.

Three mistakes to avoid

  • “Payment due on receipt.” It sounds firm and means nothing — no client pays the same day, and you have no date to chase against. Use a real date.
  • Terms that appear only on the invoice. If the client first sees your terms after the work is done, they can fairly say they never agreed to them. Put them on the quote too.
  • A late fee you never charge. A fee you waive every time is not a term, it is decoration. Either charge it when it applies, or leave it out and chase faster instead.
